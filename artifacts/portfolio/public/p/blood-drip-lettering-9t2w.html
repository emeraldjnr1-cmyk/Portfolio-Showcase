02 · Your problem

Horror on screen, a blob on the tee.

"I need a designer to create blood-dripping lettering for my clothing brand. The typography should be suitable for use on apparel and reflect a bold, horror-inspired visual direction."

Your post, Behance · Deliverable: "Production-ready artwork suitable for apparel printing"
Pain 01

Thin drips and tiny droplets do not survive ink

Most drip lettering is drawn to look good on a screen. Hairline drips break up on the press and specks of blood either disappear or print as dust around the word.

Cost today: the drips that made it horror are the first thing lost when it is printed.
Pain 02

Glossy gradients need halftones

Shiny, shaded blood is built from gradients. A screen printer has to turn those into halftone dots, and on DTG a dark red fade sinks into a dark garment.

Cost today: more screens to set up, or a muddy red that looks nothing like the file.
Pain 03

The word closes up at chest size

Ink spreads a little on fabric. Tight gaps inside the letters fill in, and a word that reads at full screen becomes one red shape on a dark tee.

Cost today: a brand name nobody can read from across the room.
Pain 04

"Production-ready" often means one PNG

A printer needs vector artwork, each colour on its own layer, the print size and the ink colours. A single flat image sends you back to the designer every time you print.

Cost today: back and forth with your printer before a single tee is made.
